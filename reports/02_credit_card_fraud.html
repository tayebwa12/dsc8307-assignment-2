Aim of the project¶

The aim is to rank transactions for a limited fraud-investigation queue. An unusual transaction deserves attention, but its anomaly score alone does not establish fraud.

Three approaches are compared: a robust statistical outlier baseline, a K-means distance detector and a one-class SVM. The SVM learns a boundary around common transactions without using fraud labels during training. This keeps the comparison consistent with the assignment's requirement to evaluate anomaly rankings against withheld labels.

Records are ordered by Time and split into roughly 60% training, 20% validation and 20% test. Repeated full feature records are removed without consulting Class. Preprocessing and models use training features only. The primary settings are fixed at K=32 and an RBF one-class SVM with nu=.01 and gamma='scale'. Validation labels are used for sensitivity analysis; test labels are reserved for final evaluation. Labels are never used to resample the data or select features.

Average precision (AP), precision and recall at a 0.5% investigation budget are the main measures. ROC-AUC is also reported, but the large number of legitimate transactions makes it insufficient on its own. Fraud prevalence provides the usual random-ranking precision–recall reference; finite-sample random AP is not exactly equal to prevalence.

Data access: The first run downloads the public benchmark data using download_data.py. The helper verifies the assignment data checksum before analysis. Internet access is required on the first run; subsequent runs use cached files. Raw data and individual customer/transaction exports are excluded from Git; published outputs contain aggregate tables and figures.

Understanding and preparing the data¶

The dataset covers two days of European card transactions. V1 to V28 are anonymized PCA components, Time records elapsed seconds and Amount is measured in EUR. The checks below examine missing values, repeated records, amount skew, component tails and changes in transaction volume.

The original variables behind the PCA components are unavailable. It is also unclear how the supplied PCA transformation was fitted, so any earlier use of the full dataset cannot be reversed. All additional transformations in this analysis are fitted on training data only.

Duplicate removal uses every recorded feature, including Time, but excludes Class. The first occurrence is retained. Similar transactions at different times can remain, and even identical records could represent separate real purchases. The evaluation therefore concerns unique full recorded patterns rather than every transaction in the original file. Removed records and split sizes are reported explicitly.

Time determines the chronological split but is excluded from model distances: otherwise later transactions could appear unusual simply because they occurred later. Amount is transformed with log1p, and all model inputs are scaled using training medians and interquartile ranges. Extreme values are retained because they may carry the anomaly signal.

A statistical baseline¶

The baseline calculates an absolute modified z-score for each feature:

0.67448975 × |value − training median| / training MAD

The largest score across the 29 inputs becomes the transaction's anomaly score. Any zero MAD is replaced by 1 as a numerical safeguard, with the number of affected columns reported.

The conventional threshold of 3.5 identifies potential statistical outliers. Applying it across many features may generate too many alerts, so its workload is compared with ranking at a fixed investigation budget. Heavy tails and correlations also make a simple Gaussian interpretation unreliable. The score is a transparent starting point, not an estimated probability of fraud.

Why use clustering and a one-class classifier?¶

Cluster-based approach. MiniBatchKMeans summarizes common transaction patterns with a manageable number of centers. Each transaction is scored by its distance to the nearest center, divided by that cluster's median training radius. This accounts for differences in the natural spread of the groups. A small numerical floor prevents division by zero. One limitation is that a compact group of fraudulent transactions could form its own cluster and no longer look unusual.

Classification-based approach. The one-class SVM uses an RBF kernel to learn a nonlinear boundary around common transactions. The negative decision-function value is used so that larger scores mean more unusual transactions. Exact kernel fitting is expensive, so the model uses a fixed random sample of 12,000 training records, selected without labels, and scores held-out data in batches.

Both methods assume that most training transactions are legitimate. Fraud in the training mixture may affect what they learn. The SVM's nu parameter controls the fitted boundary; it is not the fraud rate or the investigation budget. Neither method needs artificial fraud labels.

def fit_cluster(k,seed=SEED):
    model=MiniBatchKMeans(n_clusters=k,batch_size=4096,n_init=10,random_state=seed).fit(Atrain)
    group=model.predict(Atrain)
    distance=np.linalg.norm(Atrain-model.cluster_centers_[group],axis=1)
    radii=np.array([np.median(distance[group==j]) if np.any(group==j) else np.median(distance) for j in range(k)])
    radii=np.maximum(radii,1e-8)
    def score(A):
        g=model.predict(A)
        return np.linalg.norm(A-model.cluster_centers_[g],axis=1)/radii[g]
    return model,score
km,cluster_score=fit_cluster(32)
scores_val['Cluster distance']=cluster_score(Aval)

Evaluating the withheld labels¶

Labels are now used to measure how well each ranking identifies fraud. AP weights precision by increases in recall; it is different from the trapezoidal area under a precision–recall curve. For a random investigation queue, expected precision equals the split's fraud prevalence.

A fixed queue keeps the review workload constant. A fixed score threshold behaves differently: if transactions change over time, its alert rate can rise or fall. Both are reported. Tied scores are resolved in stable row order when forming the queue.

The validation checks use K=16, 32 and 64, and SVM nu=.005, .01 and .02. The final test comparison retains the original settings. This avoids changing the reported model after seeing a favorable test result.

How reliable are the results?¶

The test period is divided into four successive blocks to check whether AP changes with time. A paired bootstrap with 200 resamples also compares SVM AP with cluster AP on the same sampled transactions.

The bootstrap interval is descriptive. Transactions may be related, so resampling individual rows can understate uncertainty. The short collection period and missing customer identifiers also prevent a strong test of performance across customers or future weeks. Score ties and validation-to-test shifts are reported as additional checks.

Findings from the executed experiment¶

The highest final-test AP among the prespecified methods is Cluster distance, with AP 0.0393 against prevalence reference 0.0013. At a 0.5% queue, it captures 26 frauds with 258 legitimate alerts (precision 9.2%, recall 35.1%). This is a retrospective comparison, not an unbiased estimate of a winner selected on test data. For deployment, select a policy on a separate validation period and audit it on a new future period.

The tables and capacity curves show whether a method's global ranking advantage also helps at the operational budget. A detector can have attractive ROC-AUC and still generate many false investigations. Threshold alert rates should be compared with capacity rather than assumed to remain 0.5%.

Discussion of the findings¶

At the 0.5% review budget, clustering reaches 9.15% precision and 35.14% recall. The SVM reaches 4.58% precision and 17.57% recall. Clustering therefore finds twice as many frauds at this workload, but 90.85% of its alerts are still legitimate. A practical review process would need to account for that burden.

ROC-AUC alone gives a different impression: the SVM scores 0.929, compared with 0.708 for clustering. This is why the small-queue measures matter. Their AP values are close, at 0.0387 and 0.0393 respectively. The bootstrap interval for SVM minus cluster AP runs from −0.0224 to +0.0178, spanning zero. The evidence does not support a clear overall AP advantage, and the time blocks show that performance varies.

The statistical threshold of 3.5 catches 70 of 74 frauds, but also flags 17,212 legitimate transactions. That is more than 30% of the test period and is far beyond the assumed review capacity. At the much smaller 0.5% queue, its ranking finds only one fraud. The most extreme values in individual features are mostly legitimate in this test period.

The validation results suggest that the number of clusters matters. K=64 gives AP 0.0895, compared with 0.0281 for the fixed K=32 reference. K=32 was chosen as a moderate number of transaction prototypes, not as an established optimum. The K=64 result would justify a further model-selection exercise followed by a new holdout evaluation. It is not substituted into the test comparison after that test has already been opened. Changing SVM nu has little effect in this grid; gamma, feature weighting and the training sample remain possible areas for further work.

Fraud prevalence changes from 0.201% in training to 0.100% in validation and 0.130% in test. The fixed validation thresholds also produce only about 0.25–0.29% test alerts, rather than 0.5%. A threshold calibrated in one period cannot be assumed to preserve workload in another. Ranking a current batch handles a strict capacity limit; a real-time service would also need threshold and volume monitoring.

The original file has 492 fraud records, while the deduplicated splits contain 473. Dropped records are not used for training or evaluation. The conclusions apply to this data policy and short observation period. An all-record sensitivity check, customer-level validation and a longer future sample would provide stronger evidence.

Why unusual transactions are not always fraudulent¶

A large legitimate purchase, travel or a change in shopping habits can be rare. Fraud can also look ordinary if an offender imitates normal transaction patterns. These methods measure unusual feature patterns; confirming fraud requires evidence that a transaction was unauthorized. The legitimate transactions in the alert queues demonstrate that distinction.

The rankings are most suitable for investigation and proportionate verification. They should not be used alone to accuse a customer or automatically decline a payment. Confirmed review outcomes can help improve the system, but unreviewed transactions may still contain fraud, creating verification bias.

The data do not support an assessment of demographic fairness or repeated alerts against the same customer. Two days of European transactions from 2013 also cannot establish current performance in Uganda or another payment system. Finally, no investigation costs or fraud-loss model were supplied, so the analysis cannot identify the most profitable operating threshold.
